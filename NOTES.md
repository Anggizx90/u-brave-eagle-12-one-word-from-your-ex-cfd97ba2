# One Word From Your Ex — shortlist & scoring

Competition: u-brave-eagle-12-one-word-from-your-ex-cfd97ba2
Judge: z-ai/glm-5.3-flash, pairwise, criteria IN ORDER:
1. exactly one word a real ex would say about a partner they once had
2. makes the judge laugh out loud (surprise, sharp turn, lands harder than expected)
3. stings with recognition — painfully true, entrant plainly knows it
4. lands instantly, no explanation, irreplaceable by a near synonym
5. relatable — the kind of word people repeat because they have lived it too

A word that stings because it is true beats a word that only insults.
Meanness for its own sake does not count.

Scores are 1–5 per criterion (5 = best). The winner must beat runners-up
on the EARLIEST criterion where they differ.

## Winner: "fine"

Why it wins, criterion by criterion:

1. It is exactly the word an ex uses. Not a metaphor the entrant invented,
   not a status, not a phrase with a missing half — "he was fine" is a
   complete, natural ex sentence, and "fine" is the word doing all the work.
2. The laugh comes from the sharpest turn in the shortlist: anticlimax.
   The judge is braced for drama and gets the smallest verdict possible —
   and that smallness is exactly what makes it land harder than any insult.
   "Fine" is famously loaded in relationship language ("I'm fine" never
   means fine), so the joke is already inside the word.
3. It stings because it is true and the entrant knows it: to be summed up
   by someone who once loved you as merely "fine" is the most painfully
   honest verdict there is. It says you were never special — and the
   entrant submitting it is confessing exactly that.
4. It lands instantly and cannot be swapped: "okay" is neutral, "decent"
   is kind, "alright" is nothing. Only "fine", in an ex's mouth, is damning.
5. It is universal: everyone has been "fine", or called someone "fine",
   and everyone repeats the word for exactly that reason.

Runner-up: "expired" (bigger raw laugh, but weaker on C1 — an ex wouldn't
naturally call a person "expired"). Second runner-up: "almost" (truer
poetry, softer laugh — loses on C2). "Fine" beats both on the earliest
criterion where they differ.

Also: 4 bytes — the tie-break favours the smaller text.
